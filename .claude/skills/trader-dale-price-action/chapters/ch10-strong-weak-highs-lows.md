# Chapter 10: Strong or Weak Highs/Lows

## Core Idea
Not all swing highs/lows are equal: strong ones (formed by fast, aggressive rejection) are real support/resistance you should trade near and trust; weak ones (formed slowly, without aggression) are places price is likely to test through and shoot past — this distinction is a filter applied to every trade, not a standalone strategy.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Strong high/low**: a swing point formed by a fast, aggressive change of direction — evidence of aggressive rejection by buyers or sellers.
  - When to use: as confirmation that a level near your trade is real support/resistance; also as a profit-protection signal (exit before price runs into a strong high/low against your position).
  - How to identify: look for long tails / fast reversal (pin-bar-like, but not limited to that exact candle shape) — the key criterion is *aggression*, visible on more than one timeframe, not a specific candle name.
- **Weak high/low**: a swing point formed slowly and reluctantly, with many candles "testing" the same area but no single candle pushing decisively beyond the rest.
  - When to use: as a warning sign — don't treat a weak high/low as real S/R, and don't place a trade level near one on the side that would get "attracted through" it.
  - How to identify: multiple candles clustering near the same level with no aggressive rejection candle among them.

## Key Concepts
- **Aggression as the sorting criterion**: the same aggression concept from Ch2–3 determines strong vs. weak — not candle pattern names, which the author again explicitly downplays.
- **Danger zone**: a strong high/low against your position's direction — a place to consider exiting early to protect profit before price reaches it.
- **Attraction through weak points**: price tends to retest and shoot through weak highs/lows because there was no real aggressive rejection there the first time; the market "wants" to test whether anyone will trade beyond that level, generating more executable volume for institutions/market makers.

## Mental Models
- Use strong highs/lows as places to anchor your trading levels — the same aggressive participants who rejected price there are likely to help defend your position.
- Use weak highs/lows as a red flag: a weak low below your long level, or a weak high above your short level, means price is likely to get "pulled" past your level before reversing (or may not reverse at all) — skip that trade setup.
- Think of a weak zone as un-fought territory the market has an incentive to revisit and test, since more trading volume there benefits institutions and market makers pairing orders.

## Anti-patterns
- **Fading a weak high/low**: going short from a weak high or exiting a long early because of one is a common novice mistake — weak zones are meant to be tested through, not defended.
- **Placing a trade level near a weak point on the vulnerable side**: e.g., a weak low sitting below your long entry attracts price downward before (or instead of) reversing — the author explicitly avoids such setups.
- **Requiring a specific candle pattern (e.g., pin bar) to call a level "strong"**: the criterion is aggression/speed of reversal, visible across timeframes — not one candle shape.

## Worked Example
The author places long trade levels specifically where a strong low (aggressive rejection, pin-bar-like tail) is visible just below entry — reasoning that the same aggressive buyers who created that strong low will help defend the position. Conversely, when evaluating a short-level candidate, he checks for a weak high sitting just above it; seeing one, he passes on the trade, because the example chart shows price later being "attracted" through that weak high and continuing higher rather than reversing — confirming that weak zones get tested through rather than respected. In a separate case, being in a profitable long position and seeing price approaching a known strong high (aggressive prior seller rejection) ahead, he exits the position before price reaches that danger zone rather than risking the strong resistance reversing his trade.

## Key Takeaways
1. Sort every candidate swing high/low into "strong" (aggressive, fast reversal) or "weak" (slow, no decisive rejection candle) before trusting it as S/R.
2. Anchor trade levels near strong highs/lows — aggressive participants there tend to defend the level again.
3. Avoid trade levels with a weak high/low on the vulnerable side — price is likely to test through it.
4. Use a strong high/low ahead of your position as an early-exit / profit-protection signal, even before it's actually tested.
5. This is a filter applied inside every trade decision, not a standalone strategy.
