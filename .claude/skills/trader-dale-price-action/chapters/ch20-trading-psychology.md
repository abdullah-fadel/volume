# Chapter 20: Trading Psychology

## Core Idea
What matters isn't whether a trade won or lost, but whether it was properly analyzed and executed according to plan — sorting every trade into one of four categories (good win, bad win, good loss, bad loss) reframes discipline, not outcome, as the real measure of trading performance.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Four Kinds of Trades**: the author's core reframe for evaluating trades.
  - **Good winning trade**: proper analysis + flawless plan execution + wins. The trade everyone wants and the only kind worth feeling good about.
  - **Bad winning trade**: poor/no analysis, gut-feeling entry, or broken rules — but happens to win. Called out as "the worst" because it reinforces bad process (you're rewarded for breaking rules, so you break more rules, size up, and eventually take a margin call).
  - **Good losing trade** ("the hope-killer"): proper analysis + flawless execution, but still loses — because even a good strategy with a ~60% win rate will lose ~40% of the time, and streaks of losses are statistically normal. The point: don't judge process by short-term outcome.
  - **Bad losing trade**: poor/no analysis or broken discipline, and it loses. Framed constructively for beginners as immediate, cheap tuition ("a scholarship fee") — the lesson is to not repeat it, not to feel ashamed.
- **Rule-adherence via trading journal**: to stop cutting winners short (a named common failure — closing at 8-9 pips of a 10-pip target out of fear), keep a log comparing actual results to what the result *would have been* if rules were followed exactly. The accumulated statistical gap becomes the argument against future rule-bending that "feeling" alone can't win.
- **Cycle of Doom and Despair**: the failure loop where a trader abandons a strategy before mastering it, jumps to another, and repeats — never spending enough time with any one approach to know if it actually works. Exit: pick one strategy that feels most natural, commit, and give it real time. (Trying different *styles* early on to find fit is fine; jumping between many strategies once committed is not.)
- **Drawdown response tiers**:
  - **Standard drawdown**: within the range your own statistics/backtest have already shown — continue trading as usual, don't touch the strategy or money management.
  - **Excessive drawdown**: current drawdown exceeds your worst historical drawdown by 30-50%+ (e.g., if worst-ever was 20%, act once you're 26-30%+ down). Response: don't stop trading (you'd risk missing the recovery), instead cut position size (e.g., half), regain clear thinking, and only return to full size once you've recovered roughly halfway out of the drawdown and feel confident again.
- **Getting back on track via trade screenshots**: take a screenshot with brief commentary after every trade (wins and losses). When struggling, review screenshots from a period when things were working to identify what changed, and — if needed — temporarily trade only your best-performing instrument (author's example: EUR/USD) until a major portion of lost capital is recovered.

## Key Concepts
- **Real-money requirement for learning psychology**: demo accounts don't produce real fear/greed; even a very small real position (e.g., 0.01 lot) generates genuinely useful emotional data that demo trading cannot.
- **"Watch the ball, not the scoreboard"**: the author's guiding quote for staying process-focused rather than result-focused trade-to-trade.
- **Winning-streak overconfidence spiral**: success often triggers oversized risk-taking, skipped analysis, and rule-bending — precisely when vigilance should increase, not decrease.

## Mental Models
- Judge every trade by process quality (analysis + execution vs. plan), not by win/loss outcome — a loss following perfect process is not evidence the strategy is broken.
- Treat a real (even tiny) live account as mandatory lab equipment for learning trading psychology — demo experience doesn't transfer.
- During excessive drawdown, think "reduce size to regain clarity," not "stop trading" or "abandon the strategy."

## Anti-patterns
- **Judging a strategy by outcome instead of process**: leads to abandoning good strategies after a normal losing streak (the "hope-killer" trap) or reinforcing bad habits after a lucky win.
- **Cutting winners short out of fear**: closing near — but before — the planned Profit Target; the author's fix is journal-based statistical proof of the cost.
- **Jumping between strategies before mastering any one of them**: the Cycle of Doom and Despair, named by the author as a primary reason traders fail.
- **Increasing risk/position size during a winning streak out of overconfidence**: the described pattern that turns winning streaks into subsequent blowups.
- **Stopping trading entirely during an excessive drawdown**: risks missing the eventual recovery streak; reduce size instead of quitting.
- **Only screenshotting losing trades**: misses the equally important information about what's working.

## Key Takeaways
1. Sort every trade as good-win / bad-win / good-loss / bad-loss — discipline and process quality are what to optimize, not the win/loss outcome itself.
2. Use a real (even tiny) live account, not demo, to actually train trading psychology.
3. Keep a rules-vs-actual-results journal to build hard evidence against emotional rule-bending.
4. Commit to one strategy long enough to properly evaluate it — trying many styles is fine early on, but abandoning strategies mid-evaluation is the Cycle of Doom and Despair.
5. Stay extra vigilant (not less) during winning streaks; cut size (not activity) during excessive drawdowns, defined as 30-50%+ beyond your worst historical drawdown.
6. Screenshot every trade with commentary — it's the fastest way to diagnose "what changed" when performance slips, and a source of motivation when reviewed over time.
