# Chapter 2: Price Action Fundamentals

## Core Idea
Price moves because aggressive market participants use market orders to get filled *now*; those participants are overwhelmingly institutions (≈80% of currency volume via ~10 institutions, ≈96.5% of Bitcoin held by ~4% of addresses), so the goal of Price Action is to read the footprints those institutions leave on the naked chart.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Aggression drives price**: price does not move because there are "more buyers than sellers" — it moves because one side is using MARKET ORDERS aggressively to get filled immediately, absorbing available liquidity and pushing price through it.
  - When to use: as the lens for every Price Action read in the book — ask "who was being aggressive here, and why?" rather than "was this a bullish/bearish candle?"
  - How: picture a large player (e.g., a hedge fund entering 1,000 lots on a news catalyst) needing to fill NOW; the order gets filled in pieces as price runs through resting liquidity, which is what produces a fast directional move.
- **Price Action as sentences, not letters**: the author explicitly rejects candlestick-pattern reading (single "letters/words") in favor of reading continuous price dynamics/flow ("sentences") — this is the stated point of difference from typical Price Action courses.
  - When to use: whenever tempted to trade a single candlestick pattern in isolation.
  - How: assess the flow of several bars/swings together (build-up, initiation, rejection) rather than one isolated candle shape.

## Key Concepts
- **Market order**: an order filled immediately at the best available price, regardless of level — the mechanism aggressive participants use to guarantee immediate execution.
- **Institutional dominance**: ~80% of currency volume is transacted by roughly ten financial institutions; a comparable concentration exists in crypto and other asset classes.
- **Indicators as lagging derivatives**: nearly all standard indicators are mathematical transformations of only two inputs — time and historical price — so they describe the past, not the future.
- **Retail share**: retail traders control a small minority of volume across markets, reinforcing that the market is institution-driven.

## Mental Models
- Use price and volume, not indicators, when trying to infer what institutions are doing right now — indicators are just repackaged history.
- Think of an indicator-based broker analyst as an incentive-driven content producer, not a forecaster — their job is to generate trading activity, not accurate predictions.
- It's fine to use a simple indicator like EMA as a rough trend-visual aid, but never build entry/exit strategies on indicator signals — the author states this failed for him repeatedly.

## Anti-patterns
- **Trading candlestick patterns in isolation**: treats price like isolated "words" instead of the "sentence" of continuous order flow; misses the aggression/context that actually explains the move.
- **Building strategies on indicator signals**: indicators only encode time and historical price, so they cannot lead price — the author tried this extensively before abandoning it.
- **Assuming "more buyers than sellers" moves price**: this common retail/media explanation is wrong; every transaction has both a buyer and a seller, so the true driver is which side is being aggressive (using market orders), not raw counts.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask "who is being aggressive and why" instead of "is this candle bullish or bearish."
2. Read price as continuous flow (sentences), not isolated candlestick patterns (letters).
3. Institutions dominate volume across forex, crypto, and other markets — track their footprints, don't try to out-predict the news cycle.
4. Indicators are safe only as passive visual aids (e.g., trend direction via EMA); never as a strategy's signal source.
