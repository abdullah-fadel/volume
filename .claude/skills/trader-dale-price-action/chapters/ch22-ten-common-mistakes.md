# Chapter 22: The 10 Most Common Trading Mistakes You Should Avoid

## Core Idea
Most retail traders fail for a small, repeatable set of process and discipline reasons — not because good setups don't exist — and simply avoiding these ten mistakes puts a trader ahead of most of the retail field.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The 10 Mistakes** (author's checklist):
  1. **Using indicators**: reiterates Ch2 — indicators only encode price and time, so they're always lagging; there's no "magic combination" that fixes this.
  2. **Martingale**: doubling position size after every loss to recover past losses plus a small profit on the eventual win. Looks safe short-term but is mathematically guaranteed to eventually blow up on a long losing streak (worked example: starting at $10, by the 20th consecutive loss you'd need a $5,242,880 bet, already $10,485,750 in the hole; documented casino streaks have hit 32-in-a-row). Recognizable by an equity curve with one final catastrophic dip after a series of small gains.
  3. **Believing too much in one trade**: even a well-analyzed "perfect" setup is still probabilistic — a 70% win-rate level is excellent but still means real risk of loss; size discipline shouldn't loosen just because a trade "feels" certain.
  4. **Using too big positions**: oversized risk (author's example: 10% per trade) makes it psychologically impossible to think clearly after a losing streak, which leads to rule-bending and compounding the damage. Rule of thumb: size positions so you'd still feel mentally stable after a realistic losing streak.
  5. **Never being able to admit you were wrong**: without a predetermined Stop-loss, traders who are "in the red" tend to freeze and hope rather than act — usually ending in a worse outcome than a planned exit would have produced.
  6. **Not using a Stop-loss**: called out as "probably the dumbest thing you can do in trading" — removes all risk control; a so-called "mental SL" fails for the same reason as #5 (traders don't honor it), and also leaves you exposed to sudden unexpected spikes (bank intervention, disaster, political shock) that can wipe an account before manual action is possible.
  7. **Entering a position without a plan**: every trade needs a predefined PT, SL, and management plan before entry; the author flags "gut-feeling" entries chasing a fast post-news move as the most common trigger for this mistake.
  8. **Following other people's ideas blindly**: distinguishes healthy learning from an experienced mentor from copying trade ideas (especially from broker analysts, whose incentive is to generate trading volume, not to make the client money) — if you follow someone's idea, you must independently understand and agree with the logic, and know your own PT/SL/management before entering.
  9. **Jumping from strategy to strategy**: restates the Cycle of Doom and Despair (Ch20) — no strategy succeeds without sustained time, patience, and practice; strategy-hopping guarantees losses and frustration.
  10. **Sticking with a bad broker**: underestimating the cost of wide spreads and slippage; the author states that switching from a bad broker to a good one "can be a matter of turning constant losing into a consistent winning," and that intraday trading with a 2-3 pip spread on major pairs isn't viable at all.

## Key Concepts
- **Martingale**: a bet-doubling-after-loss money management system borrowed from casino gambling; guaranteed to fail eventually regardless of edge, because loss streaks of any length are always possible given enough trades.
- **Mental Stop-loss**: an unenforced, manually-triggered exit level — functionally unreliable because it depends on a trader's in-the-moment discipline, the exact failure mode this mistake is meant to avoid.
- **Broker cost impact**: spread/slippage differences of even a pip or two can be the deciding factor between a winning and losing trade at the level precision this book's strategies require.

## Mental Models
- Treat any money-management system that "doubles down after a loss" as a red flag regardless of how it's dressed up — the failure mode is mathematical, not a matter of execution skill.
- Size every position for the losing streak you'll eventually have, not the winning streak you hope for.
- Treat broker selection as a trading-edge decision, not an administrative afterthought — tight spreads directly determine whether a level-based strategy is viable at all.

## Key Takeaways
1. Never use martingale or any loss-doubling money management — it fails with mathematical certainty given enough trades.
2. Always use a predetermined, honored Stop-loss on every trade — never a "mental" one.
3. Size positions for post-losing-streak clarity, not best-case gains.
4. Treat even high-probability (e.g., 70% win-rate) setups as probabilistic, never certain.
5. Vet any followed trading idea (especially from broker analysts) against your own independent analysis and plan.
6. Commit to one strategy long enough to properly evaluate it rather than hopping between strategies.
7. Choose a broker with tight spreads deliberately — it can be the difference between a systematically winning and systematically losing result.
