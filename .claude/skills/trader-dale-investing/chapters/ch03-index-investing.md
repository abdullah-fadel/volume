# Chapter 3: Index Investing

## Core Idea
Buying a stock index is the simplest, lowest-effort way to invest — one purchase buys a proportional slice of every company in the basket — but the index's sector composition (especially heavy Financial/Technology weighting) is a hidden risk the author explicitly filters against in his own approach.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Value-weighted index calculation**: most major indexes weight constituents by market capitalization, so bigger companies move the index far more than smaller ones (e.g., Apple ~6-7% of the S&P 500 vs. Electronic Arts ~0.1% — an Apple move swings the whole index; an EA move barely registers).
  - When to use: before trusting an index's "diversification," check what's actually driving it.
  - How: look up a sector heatmap (e.g., finviz.com/map.ashx, marketscreener.com heatmaps) to see which companies/sectors dominate the basket by tile size.
- **Choosing a country/index — two-check framework**: before investing in *any* country's index, check (1) **Interest rates** and (2) **Sovereign credit rating**.
  - Interest rates: avoid economies whose growth depends on artificial stimulus (zero/negative rates held for years — USA, Eurozone, Japan are cited examples). Rule of thumb: don't invest in an economy "addicted" to cheap-money stimulus, since the eventual withdrawal of that stimulus risks a crash.
  - Rating: check the "Big Three" agencies (Moody's, S&P, Fitch). Look only for countries at "non-investment grade (speculative)" or better — this filters out the riskiest sovereign risk, though the author cautions ratings agencies missed the 2008 crisis and shouldn't be trusted blindly.

## Key Concepts
- **Blue chips**: the large, stable companies that dominate major indexes — lower single-company risk than picking unproven small caps, though blue chips can still fail.
- **Sector concentration risk**: the index's single biggest structural weakness — Financial and Technology sectors often make up ~40%+ of major indexes (S&P 500) and both sectors fall first and hardest in a crisis.
- **Heatmap tools**: countryeconomy.com/stock-exchange and markets.ft.com/data/world to find/compare indexes; marketscreener.com for sector-weight heatmaps of any chosen index.

## Mental Models
- Use interest rates as a "drug dependency" test for an economy: a booming index built on near-zero rates is a patient on stimulants, not a healthy patient — the boom is real, but not sustainable once withdrawal begins.
- Treat sovereign ratings as one data point among several, not a verdict — the author notes some AAA-rated countries carry enormous debt and stimulus dependence, undercutting the rating's face value.

## Anti-patterns
- **Buying whichever index is "the biggest" without checking composition**: you may be unknowingly over-exposed to the exact sectors (Financial, Technology) the author considers riskiest.
- **Trusting a country's rating as a complete risk picture**: ratings agencies are independent but imperfect (missed 2008); use rating as a screening filter, not a final answer.
- **Investing in an index already near its all-time high just because the underlying country "looks good" fundamentally**: the author explicitly passed on Australia's ASX despite liking its fundamentals (heavy Basic Materials weighting, AAA rating, low debt) because it was near its price maximum — price/timing still matters even for index investing.

## Worked Example
The author compares indexes across countries via sector heatmaps: the S&P 500 (USA) and Hang Seng (China, over 70% Financials + Technology) both carry heavy exposure to his two least-favorite sectors; DAX (Germany) and BOVESPA (Brazil) carry comparatively little Technology weight. His own preferred profile is exemplified by the ASX 200 (Australia): dominated by Basic Materials (a sector he *does* like), AAA rating, positive interest rates, low relative debt, ~5% unemployment — fundamentally his ideal index — yet he still declined to buy it at the time because it was trading near its historical maximum. The lesson: pass the country/index through the fundamental filter (rates + rating + sector mix) first, then still wait for price to offer a discount before buying, exactly as with individual stocks later in the book.

## Key Takeaways
1. Index investing needs no stock-picking skill, has no management fee, requires very little capital, and is structurally protected against inflation — but you cannot cherry-pick out the sectors/companies you dislike.
2. Because indexes are value-weighted, always check *what* actually drives the index (via a heatmap) before assuming it's diversified.
3. Screen any country/index candidate through interest rates (avoid stimulus-dependent economies) and credit rating (non-investment grade or better) before considering it.
4. Even a fundamentally ideal index should still be bought at a discount, not at its all-time high — fundamentals qualify a candidate; price/timing decides when to act.
