# Chapter 13: The Most Common Mistakes

## Core Idea
Nearly every recurring investor mistake the author sees is a failure of *discipline* after a sound plan was already made — extrapolating the past, chasing highs, overreacting to news, or second-guessing a limit order at the last minute — not a failure of analysis.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Eleven common mistakes, with the author's fix for each**:
  1. **Don't extrapolate the past**: assuming a chart (an index, a range-bound stock) will keep behaving as it has is unreliable — conditions change (e.g., S&P 500's post-2010s rise was stimulus-driven and not guaranteed to continue as rates normalize).
  2. **Don't buy at all-time highs**: amateur investors buy at highs from FOMO/greed; fund managers are often *forced* to (their mandates require staying near-fully invested) — this book's whole method exists to avoid both traps by waiting for a Volume-Profile discount instead.
  3. **Don't invest in Pink Sheets or Penny Stocks**: roughly 1-in-100 succeed; viable only as a high-volume, many-bets strategy incompatible with this book's concentrated, patient approach (see Ch4).
  4. **Don't bet it all on one stock**: no matter how strong a single company looks, never abandon per-position sizing discipline (Ch12's 4% cap).
  5. **Don't invest in just one sector**: a sector-wide downturn drags down every holding in it even if individual company fundamentals are fine — diversify across at least 3+ sectors (Ch5/Ch12 recommend 4–5).
  6. **Don't check your charts/portfolio too often**: daily or multiple-times-daily checking produces false control and emotional, hasty decisions; with limit orders and alerts set, weekly review is sufficient.
  7. **Don't switch to faster timeframes**: a long-term investor who analyzed on Daily/Weekly charts should stay there — switching to 4-hour/1-hour charts introduces noise that provokes bad, short-term-framed decisions.
  8. **Don't change your predetermined time horizon**: don't cut a winning position early out of fear of "losing the gain," and don't panic-sell an underperforming-but-fundamentally-sound stock — if you built a 5–10 year plan, hold to it absent a real fundamental change (Ch11's "no bright future" rule).
  9. **Don't change your plans at the last minute**: once analysis is done and a limit order is set, execute the plan; last-minute impulse decisions ("the price is falling too fast, I should cancel" or "it's taking too long, I should buy early") are consistently poor and stem from wanting to feel in control.
  10. **Don't buy before a dividend just to sell after**: the share price typically drops by roughly the dividend amount on the ex-dividend date, and the dividend itself is taxable — this "dividend capture" idea has no real edge and only costs you commissions and tax.
  11. **Don't invest money you'll need soon**: this is a multi-year discipline; money earmarked for near-term needs (e.g., within the next year) doesn't belong in this strategy.
  - **Bonus discipline — don't be afraid to go against the crowd**: this strategy requires buying during recessions, panics, and bad news cycles — precisely when it's psychologically hardest to do so; financial media rarely frames a sell-off as a buying opportunity, so treat news-driven fear as background noise, not a signal.

## Key Concepts
- **Extrapolation**: assuming recent price behavior (a rising trend or a stable range) will simply continue — the author calls this out as a beginner reflex that markets repeatedly falsify.
- **Ex-dividend date**: the date after which a new buyer no longer receives the upcoming dividend; the stock price mechanically adjusts down by roughly the dividend amount on this date.

## Mental Models
- Treat the "planning phase" and "execution phase" of a trade as strictly separate — once a limit order is placed based on completed analysis, the only job left is to let it execute, not to keep re-analyzing in real time.
- Treat market panic and negative headlines as the entry signal, not the exit signal, for this specific strategy — going against the crowd is a structural requirement, not an occasional bonus move.

## Anti-patterns
- **Following financial news sentiment as an investing signal**: journalists and analysts systematically extrapolate recent sell-offs and problems forward, which the author explicitly says shouldn't be given much weight.
- **Switching timeframes right before entry "to catch the best moment"**: an attempt to optimize execution to the last cent that consistently backfires and defeats the low-effort premise of the whole method.

## Key Takeaways
1. Most investing mistakes are discipline failures after a good plan, not analytical failures — extrapolation, chasing highs, over-monitoring, last-minute plan changes.
2. Diversify across sectors (not just stocks) — a sector-wide downturn hits every position in it regardless of individual fundamentals.
3. Once a limit order is set from completed analysis, let it execute — don't second-guess it in real time or switch to faster timeframes right before entry.
4. Hold to your predetermined multi-year time horizon; don't cut winners early from fear or bail on sound-but-underperforming positions out of impatience.
5. This strategy structurally requires buying during fear and panic — expect it to feel uncomfortable when it's working correctly.
