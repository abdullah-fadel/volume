# Chapter 11: When to Sell Your Stocks?

## Core Idea
Selling is triggered by a *combination* of warning signals — a stock index at all-time highs, an already-large gain on the position, and a company P/E stretched well above its sector average — plus a separate, rarer trigger: the company or its industry has permanently lost its bright future, regardless of price.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three combined sell signals** (the author is explicit that no single signal alone is sufficient):
  1. **The broad index is too high**: check the relevant index for your market (S&P 500 for US stocks, Euro Stoxx 50, FTSE 100, DAX, Hang Seng, etc.) — if it's at or near an all-time high, begin scaling down exposure. Rule of thumb: at new all-time highs, hold only ~50% of funds in stocks, reducing further (even to 10–20%) as the index keeps climbing, redeploying freed capital into corporate bond ETFs while waiting for a drop.
  2. **The position has already made a solid gain**: a large realized/unrealized profit is itself a signal to consider trimming, not a reason to hold indefinitely out of greed.
  3. **The company's P/E has drifted too far above its sector average**: check the current sector-average P/E (e.g., via gurufocus.com), and treat roughly **+50% or more above that average** as a threshold to start reducing the position.
- **"No bright future" sell rule (Buffett-derived)**: sell regardless of price or index level when a company's or industry's fundamental prospects have permanently changed for the worse — cited examples: selling newspaper stocks (Washington Post) once internet media structurally replaced print, and selling airline stocks when COVID-19 permanently damaged near-term travel economics. This is a low-frequency trigger, used rarely, but overrides the price-based signals when it applies.
- **Portfolio recap discipline**: after fundamental screening (Ch5–Ch7) and Volume Profile timing (Ch9–Ch10), periodically re-run the same fundamental criteria across your holdings and prune sectors/stocks that no longer qualify, exactly as when the list was first built.

## Key Concepts
- **Scaling out, not all-at-once selling**: the recommended sell process is gradual — reduce a portion at the first warning combination, more as the signals intensify, rather than an all-or-nothing exit.
- **Redeployment while waiting**: capital freed by trimming an overextended position doesn't sit idle in cash — it moves into corporate bond ETFs (low stock correlation) until a new discount opportunity appears (own market or a different country's index/sector, per Ch3).

## Mental Models
- Treat the index level, position gain, and sector-relative P/E as three independent dials that must all be turned up together before a full sell decision — any one alone is noise.
- Use "would I still hold this if I found no bright future for the industry" as a periodic gut-check separate from the price-based rules — this is the rare override case.

## Anti-patterns
- **Selling based on P/E alone**: sector-average P/E varies too widely to be a standalone trigger; it only matters in combination with index level and position gain.
- **Holding a stock purely out of loyalty once its industry has structurally changed**: the "no bright future" rule exists precisely to override attachment to a stock that used to be a good investment.
- **Trying to time the exact top**: the framework is about *scaling down* as multiple warning signs accumulate, not predicting a single peak.

## Key Takeaways
1. Don't sell on any single signal — wait for index-at-highs, large realized gain, and sector-relative P/E overextension (~+50% above sector average) to align.
2. Scale exposure down gradually (e.g., toward 50%, then 10–20% of funds in stocks) as signals intensify, rather than exiting all at once.
3. Park freed capital in corporate bond ETFs while waiting for the next discount opportunity, rather than sitting in cash or chasing a new position immediately.
4. Reserve the "no bright future" rule for genuine structural change in a company/industry (e.g., a technology or regulatory shift that permanently damages the business) — it overrides price-based signals entirely.
5. Periodically re-apply the original fundamental screening criteria (Ch5–Ch7) to existing holdings, not just new candidates.
