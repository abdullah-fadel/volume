# Chapter 5: FINVIZ Descriptive Tab & Sector Guide

## Core Idea
Of FINVIZ's ~20 Descriptive-tab filters, only four matter for long-term investing — Market Capitalization, Dividend Yield, Sector, and Industry — and the single highest-leverage decision in this whole chapter is *which sectors to avoid entirely* (Financial and Technology, by the author's explicit judgment) before ever looking at an individual company.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The 4-filter Descriptive tab**: ignore Analyst Recommendations/Target Price (unverifiable, averaged nonsense), Average Volume and Float (day-trading metrics, irrelevant to investing), and IPO Date (optional old-company filter, not essential). Use only:
  - **Market Capitalization**: filter to at least "Small" (>$300M), prefer "Mid" and up (≥$2B) — bigger companies are more time-tested and less likely to fail outright.
  - **Dividend Yield**: filter to ≥2% (covers/offsets inflation). 2–4% is healthy and sustainable; much higher (e.g., double-digit, common in REITs) warrants suspicion — check whether it's sustainable given the sector.
  - **Sector**: FINVIZ recognizes 11 sectors; only one can be selected per screening pass, so you screen sector-by-sector.
  - **Industry**: the sub-category within a sector (e.g., Gold, Banks, Software) — used for finer targeting once a sector is chosen.
- **Sector recommendation framework**: sort all 11 sectors into Recommended vs. Not Recommended based on (a) sensitivity to the business/economic cycle, (b) barrier to entry for competitors, (c) tangible asset backing (book value), and (d) dividend reliability. Recommended: choose 4–5 sectors total for a portfolio — fewer and you're not diversified; more and you might as well buy the index (cheaper).

## Key Concepts
- **Payout ratio**: the share of earnings a company distributes as dividends vs. retains for growth — neither high nor low is inherently good; growing companies retain more, mature companies pay more.
- **Price inelastic demand**: sectors selling products people buy regardless of the economy (Healthcare, Consumer Defensive) are structurally more stable than sectors selling discretionary goods (Consumer Cyclical, Technology).
- **Book value / P/B as a "catastrophic Stop Loss"**: a sector's typical Price-to-Book ratio indicates how much money you could theoretically recover if a company went bankrupt — high-book-value sectors (Energy, Industrials, Utilities, Basic Materials) offer more downside protection than low-book-value sectors (Technology, Healthcare).

## Mental Models
- Treat "not sensitive to the business cycle" as the single strongest quality signal across the sector notes — Consumer Defensive, Healthcare, and Utilities all earn their "Recommended" status primarily through this property.
- Use dividend yield as a secondary signal of sector risk, not just income: sectors forced to pay abnormally high dividends by law (REITs, must distribute ≥90% of income) or by high leverage carry hidden fragility that the yield number alone won't show.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating a low P/E or low P/B as automatically "cheap and safe"**: the Financial sector has the *lowest* average P/E and P/B of all 11 sectors, yet the author calls it the riskiest — the low ratios reflect leverage-driven risk, not a bargain.
- **Spreading a portfolio across too many sectors**: beyond ~5 sectors, diversification benefit is marginal and you'd be cheaper off just buying the index.
- **Using P/E or P/B on Real Estate/REITs at face value**: REIT accounting depreciation distorts P/E, and book value poorly reflects real property value — use P/NAV instead for this sector.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use only 4 Descriptive-tab filters: Market Cap (≥$300M, prefer ≥$2B), Dividend Yield (≥2%), Sector, Industry.
2. Pick 4–5 sectors for a diversified but focused portfolio — not fewer, not many more.
3. The author's Recommended set: Basic Materials, Consumer Defensive, Healthcare, Industrials, Utilities. Not Recommended: Financial, Technology, Consumer Cyclical (partially).
4. A sector's normal P/E, P/B, and Debt/Equity vary enormously — always judge a company's ratios *against its own sector's typical range*, never against a universal number.
5. REITs/Real Estate need P/NAV, not P/E or P/B, because of depreciation accounting distortion.
